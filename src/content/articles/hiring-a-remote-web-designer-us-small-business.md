More and more small businesses in the United States work with web designers they never meet in person. The designer might be in the next state, across the country, or across the border in Canada. Everything from the first call to the final launch can happen online.

Working remotely can give you more choice. You are no longer limited to the agencies in your own town, so you can pick the team whose work, communication style and pricing fit your business best.

The trade-off is that you need to ask a few more questions up front. A designer down the street can explain things over coffee. With a remote designer, clear answers in writing matter even more.

Here are 10 questions worth asking before you hire anyone to build your website, wherever they are based.

## 1. Who will own my domain, website and accounts?

This is the most important question on the list. Your domain name (yourbusiness.com), your hosting account, your Google Business Profile and your analytics should be registered in your business's name, or at least be fully transferable to you.

If a designer registers everything under their own account, you can end up locked in. Leaving might mean losing your web address, your search history or your reviews.

**What a good answer sounds like:** "You own your domain and accounts. We'll ask you to add us as a manager or user, and you can remove our access whenever you like."

## 2. What time zone do you work in, and when can we talk?

Time zones matter less than most people expect, but you should know them in advance. A designer on Pacific Time shares a working day with the West Coast and overlaps easily with Mountain and Central Time. For East Coast businesses, mornings on their side line up with late morning and afternoon on yours.

Ask how quickly they usually reply to email, and whether you can book calls at times that suit you.

## 3. What currency are your prices in?

If your designer is outside the US, their prices may be in another currency, such as Canadian dollars (CAD). In that case, your card provider converts the amount into US dollars on the day you pay, using its own exchange rate.

Some cards charge a foreign transaction fee for payments to a business in another country. That fee comes from your bank, not the designer, so it is worth checking with your card provider before you start.

**What a good answer sounds like:** "Our prices are in CAD. Your quote and every invoice show the exact amount before you pay."

## 4. What exactly is included in the price?

Ask for a written quote that lists what you are getting. Useful things to check:

- How many pages the site will have.
- Whether writing, photos or a logo are included or extra.
- How many rounds of changes you can ask for.
- Whether basic SEO setup is part of the build.
- What happens to the price if you add something later.

A clear quote protects both sides. Vague quotes are where surprise bills come from.

## 5. Will my website be custom or based on a template?

There is nothing wrong with either approach, but you should know which one you are paying for. A custom-designed site is planned around your business, your customers and your goals. A template-based site can be faster and cheaper, but may look similar to many others.

Ask to see examples of sites they have built and how each one was made for that business.

## 6. How will you learn about my local market?

A remote designer can still build a site that works well in your city. The key is research. Ask how they will learn about your customers, the words people use when they search for your services, and the competitors in your area.

If local customers matter to you, ask how they handle your address, phone number, service areas and Google Business Profile. Our article on [making your small business website more trustworthy](/articles/make-your-small-business-website-trustworthy) explains why these details matter.

## 7. How do contracts, signing and payments work?

Most remote designers handle everything online. Ask:

- Will there be a written agreement, and can I read it before paying anything?
- How do I sign it? (Many teams use secure online signing.)
- How much is the deposit, and when is the rest due?
- How do I pay? Card, bank transfer or something else?

You should never feel rushed into paying before you understand what you are agreeing to.

## 8. How will I know what is happening during the project?

With no office to visit, regular updates matter. Ask how you will follow progress. Some teams send email updates, some share a project board, and some give you your own client page where you can see each step and approve work before it goes live.

## 9. Will my site be ready for phones and search engines?

Most of your visitors will likely find you on a phone, so your site should be easy to read and use on a small screen. It should also load quickly and have the basic settings that help search engines understand each page.

Ask what they include, but be careful with anyone who promises a guaranteed number one ranking on Google. No honest designer or SEO provider can guarantee that. Our guide to [what SEO setup includes](/articles/what-is-seo-setup) explains what a solid foundation looks like.

## 10. What happens after launch?

A website is not finished the day it goes live. Ask:

- Who fixes things if something breaks?
- Can I make small changes myself?
- Do you offer ongoing help, such as monthly SEO or updates, and is it optional?
- If I decide to leave, how do I take my website with me?

The last question tells you a lot. A designer who makes leaving easy is usually one who is confident you will want to stay.

## Hiring across the border: what changes and what doesn't

If you are a US business thinking about working with a Canadian team, very little changes in practice. You still get a website built for your customers and your market. The main differences are the currency on your invoices and, depending on where you are, the time difference.
